## The hard extension: refusal

They call it "the hardest thing on the page, almost nobody gets it right," and it's closest to a real product: a wrong answer given with high confidence is worse than no answer. I'd pick it and do it properly:
- **Hard negatives:** photograph items that are *not* in the catalogue but look like items that are (same brand, similar model). An unrelated object is trivial to reject; a lookalike is the real test.
- **No tuning on the test set:** set the threshold on one split, then report false-accept and false-reject rates on a separate held-out split. Most people tune and report on the same data without noticing.
- Try a raw cosine threshold first, **measure why it fails** (scores sit at different levels for different items), then replace it with something better, such as the gap between the top two scores or per-item normalization. That covers point 3.

## Problems in their own brief to find before they do

These are the "thing you noticed" items. Each can be made concrete:

- **Accuracy is ambiguous when products look identical.** Scraped catalogues contain the same shoe or frame in several listings or colours that no photo can tell apart. We detect those groups and report both exact-item accuracy and group accuracy. Otherwise the accuracy number misleads.
- **A clean baseline can accidentally cheat.** If "clean" means searching the catalogue with its own images, you get about 100% for free. The fair design: for every item, take one clean phone photo *and* several hard ones. Each failure condition is then measured against the same item's clean shot, which isolates the condition from the general studio-vs-phone gap.
- **One photo often has several failure conditions at once** (e.g. bad lighting plus a hand in frame), so a simple per-condition table mixes their effects. The paired design fixes that, and we'll say so.
- **100 photos is a small sample.** Each condition gets about 15 photos, so a result like "70%" could really be anywhere from about 50% to 90%. Report those uncertainty ranges. Almost no one does, and it shows you understand the numbers.
- **The confidence score should mean something.** Check whether 0.8 confidence is actually right about 80% of the time.

## Obvious approach tried, measured, replaced (on the matcher)

Embedding the whole photo lets clutter and hands dominate the result. Measure that on the cluttered and hand-in-frame photos, then add a step that crops to the object before embedding, and show the difference. Also compare DINOv2 with CLIP on your data rather than picking one by reputation.

Growing to 100k items, multiple items in one photo, and the automated stumper. DECISIONS.md will say why in one line each.

## Why jewellery fits Problem 2

**Their "hard conditions" are everyday jewellery conditions.** Reflections, a hand or wrist in the frame, and small objects in clutter are exactly what happens when you photograph gold on a person.

**Their products create this matching problem.**
- A WhatsApp shopper sends a photo of a friend's necklace, or a screenshot from Instagram, and asks "do you have this?"
- A shopper at the in-store counter holds up a piece.
- E-Catalog Studio exists because iPhone photos and studio photos look so different. That same gap is what breaks a matcher.

## Jewellery-specific things they didn't ask for

These are the "noticed it ourselves" material for DECISIONS.md:

1. **One design, many SKUs.** The same design is sold in 14K and 18K, yellow and rose gold, and different sizes, and no photo can tell these apart. We group listings that look identical and report exact-SKU accuracy and design-level accuracy separately.
2. **Studio shots vs model shots.** Product pages mix plain packshots with photos of the piece being worn. Which images to index, and how to combine several images into one product score, is a decision we measure rather than guess.
